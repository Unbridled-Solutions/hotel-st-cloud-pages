Prayer and health. What the studies actually show.

Two questions get mixed together. Distant prayer as a medical intervention is one. Prayer you do yourself, and a faith community you belong to, is another. The evidence is different for each.

Straight read Science has not shown that strangers praying for you, without you knowing, statistically heals the body. Prayer you do yourself is associated with better mental health. Belonging to a worshipping community is associated with longer life in the large epidemiology. Faith can sit next to medicine. It does not replace it. These trials never claimed to prove or disprove God. They only asked whether this design moved hospital charts.

1. Strangers praying for you. Physical outcomes.

The best evidence does not show a statistical win.

Cochrane review, 2009

Ten randomized trials. 7,646 patients. Distant intercessory prayer plus usual care versus usual care alone. No clear difference in death (relative risk 1.00, 95% CI 0.74 to 1.36) or general clinical state. Authors would not recommend for or against it, and were not convinced further trials of this design were worth the money.

STEP. Benson, Harvard, 2006

Largest trial. 1,802 coronary bypass patients at six U.S. hospitals. Christian groups prayed for 14 days starting the night before surgery. Primary outcome: any complication within 30 days.

Blinded prayer: complications 52% versus 51%. No benefit. The group that knew they were being prayed for did worse: 59% versus 52%. Thirty-day mortality was the same across groups (about 2.2% to 2.6%).

Two older CCU studies that looked positive

Byrd 1988 (393 patients) and Harris 1999 reported better hospital-course scores with remote prayer. Those signals did not hold when the bigger, cleaner trials ran. Cochrane folded them into the mixed picture above.

This is prayer as a drug you give a stranger without them knowing. That is the design science can blind. It is not the same as a family praying at the bedside.

2. You praying. Mental and emotional health.

Here the signal is real, and it is mostly about the person who prays.

Anderson and Nunnelley, Postgraduate Medicine, 2016

Analytical review of clinical studies on private prayer. Nine of eleven studies found frequent private prayer associated with a significantly lower prevalence of depression. Observational work also pointed to optimism, coping, and anxiety. Association, not a proof of mechanism.

Boelens et al., 2009. Randomized trial

Direct person-to-person prayer. Six weekly one-hour sessions versus none. Depression and anxiety improved versus controls (p < 0.01) and held at one month. Cortisol did not change. Small study, mostly women, adjunct to care. Not a cure. Looks a lot like what we already know about contemplative practice: less rumination, more hope, better coping.

3. Faith community, not just prayer

Harvard Nurses' Health Study. Li / VanderWeele, JAMA Internal Medicine, 2016

Women who attended religious services more than once a week had 33% lower all-cause mortality over 16 years versus never-attenders (hazard ratio 0.67, 95% CI 0.62 to 0.71), after adjusting for major lifestyle factors. That is attendance and community, not a prayer RCT. Social support, less smoking, meaning, and prayer are all tangled together.

How to hold it

  1. Prayer as a blinded medical intervention has not passed the physical-outcome test.
  2. Prayer as something you do is associated with better depression and anxiety scores in the studies that looked.
  3. A worshipping community is associated with longer life in the large epidemiology. That is the strongest physical-health number in this file, and it is not a prayer trial.
  4. None of this is a reason to skip treatment. The honest claim is accompaniment, not replacement.
