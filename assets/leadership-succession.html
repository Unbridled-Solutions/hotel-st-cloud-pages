Grow the next presidents inside the house.

Several live events presidents, including OrangeDoor, will phase out over the next three to five years. Outside hires have a weak track record here because of the culture barrier. This is what the research says, and the pathway I would run.

19 August 2026 · Confidential · Not a public site · Prepared by MC

The defining principle

Process now. Person later.

Do not name a single successor yet. Start the work this year. The path that fits Unbridled is grow inside-outsiders: people who already know the house, then stretch them until they can lead it without becoming a photocopy of the current president. If the bench is thin, bring a high-character person in now at a real operating seat and grow them for three to five years. Do not drop a finished outsider into the chair.

What the numbers say

1,800

Harvard successions studied by Joseph Bower. Companies performed significantly better when an insider became CEO.

63%

of new public-company CEOs worldwide in 2025 were internal. About 70% of FTSE 100 CEOs were too.

84%

more likely an external CEO is replaced in the first three years. About 40% of outside executive hires fail inside 18 months.

That last number is our lived experience, written as a statistic. Internal CEOs also stay longer (8.7 years vs 7.3) and get fired less (24% vs 30%). Companies with new internal CEOs outperformed their regional index by 4.4% vs 0.5% for external hires. Outside hires get paid about 15% more and get worse reviews for the first two years.

External hires make sense in a turnaround, a governance reset, or when the culture itself is the problem. None of those is the live events case. Culture is the product. Customers buy who we are.

The model that solves the culture barrier

Bower called them inside-outsiders. Inside: they know the people, the clients, the unwritten rules, and how the culture actually works. Outside: they have not drunk the Kool-Aid. They will still tell the truth about what has to change.

What works Jack Welch at GE

Grew up inside. Came from a non-core business. Stayed a maverick. Changed the company in a way the culture could absorb.

What breaks Edwin Artzt at P&G

Insider who tried to change it like an outsider and broke trust. Knowing what to change is not the same as knowing how this house can take it.

A finished outsider dropped into the chair hits the wall you already know. A loyal number two who never left the building can become a curator of the past. You want someone who loves the house and will still remodel a room.

How soon to pick

Name the successor 12 to 24 months before the handoff, not five years before. Public coronation too early starts a horse race and drains everyone else.

Now through about year 2

Quiet phase

Identify and develop two or three people. Stretch roles they could fail. Coaching. Occasional look at the open market. Do not announce a slate.

About 2 years before departure

Hot phase

Formal evaluation. Visible process. You drive it. Same questions for every candidate. Character first. Internal and external comparison.

12 to 24 months before handoff

Relay

Name the successor. Overlap. Outgoing president goes nose in, fingers out. Clients get walked across, not emailed.

McKinsey on founder and family businesses: the average succession destroys value. TSR down 5.7 points after a CEO change. Two-thirds see revenue growth drop. When it is done well, insider transitions create outsize returns. Their ideal clock is 8 to 15 years. We have 3 to 5. So start this year and compress with intent.

PwC: only about a third of US family businesses have a robust, written, communicated plan. Most plans are informal. Informal is how you get a scramble.

How to pick without starting a war

Do not pick the loyal number two by default. Pick against a forward spec: what this company must be in 2030, not who kept the trains running in 2024.

  1. Character that already matches the four cornerstones. Not "will learn the culture." Already lives it.
  2. Inside-outsider posture. Loves the house. Will still name what is broken.
  3. Relational equity with clients and the other presidents. Authority is a blunt instrument in an equity-holding ecosystem.
  4. Can enroll people, not just manage them. Same test we apply to the CCO.
  5. Proven on a stretch bigger than their current box: a new market, a P&L, a hard account, a cross-company show.
  6. The team would follow them on a bad Tuesday, not just a good Friday.

Run two or three people through the same development, not one heir. Top performers always evaluate multiple candidates, including at least a look at the external market so the internal choice is a choice, not a default. Until you are inside the 12 to 24 month window, the public story is: we are building leaders. The presidency is one of several futures in this house.

How to keep everyone else motivated

This is where most houses lose the plot. The people who do not get the chair leave, or quietly quit.

Do this
  • Develop a bench, not a crown prince. Fold stretch work into ordinary leadership development. Do not brand it "succession" for five years.
  • Give non-winners a real second life the same week you name the successor.
  • Never imply the job is already theirs, even if you have a favorite.
  • Make other seats worth staying for: equity that still matters, a COO or client-lead or new-market chair that is not a consolation prize, a season in the other country.
  • Tell the truth early to people who will not be president.
Why it works Disney, 2026

Josh D'Amaro got CEO. Runner-up Dana Walden became President and Chief Creative Officer the same day. Retention was designed, not hoped for.

Open multi-year horse races create conflict and hurt performance. Relay succession (a named heir, late) outperforms a public race, if the rest of the bench has somewhere to go.

Repeated outside hires at the top teach mid-level people that loyalty has a ceiling. People stay about twice as long where they can actually rise.

If you truly have no internal

Do not hire a finished president from a holding company and hope they get Unbridled. Three better moves, in order:

First Grow one from one level down

A director or GM with the character, given a 24 to 36 month operating runway and a mentor who is not threatened by them.

Second Move inside the ecosystem

A proven Unbridled president or number two who already lives the DNA, moved into OrangeDoor or the reverse, with a long overlap. Culture travels better across the endorsed house than across the street.

Third Import early, not late

Hire an outsider now into a real seat, not the chair. Three to five years of client work, Friday Forum, 20/20/60, and a P&L before they ever sit in the president's office. You are building an inside-outsider on purpose.

If you use a search firm, brief them on character and DNA first, credentials second. Never let them sell you an operator who has never been loved by a team.

The outgoing president

McKinsey is blunt: the exit plan is as important as the successor.

  • Write when they leave operating control. Date it.
  • Give them a next chapter: board, ambassador, UnbridledACTS, client elder, mentor. People who have no next chapter cling.
  • Nose in, fingers out. Some founders even move offices so the building can see the change.
  • Clean the house before they go: hard people decisions, pricing, org clutter. Do not hand the new person a mess and call it a gift.

This matches how Unbridled already writes leadership-transition letters: honor the person, design a role around their life, be specific.

A 3 to 5 year pathway

When What we do
Year 0 · now For each company in the window, write a one-page 2030 spec. Quietly name two or three inside-outsider candidates, including at least one who is not the obvious number two. If the slate is empty, start the three-to-five-year import now. Tell sitting presidents the truth: we are building a bench. This is stewardship, not a vote of no confidence.
Years 1 to 2 · quiet Each candidate gets a stretch they could fail. Mentorship from the sitting president and from someone outside that company. Light external benchmark once. Do not run a public search.
Year 2 to 3 · hot Formal look. Same questions for every candidate. Character first. Decide. Name the successor 12 to 24 months out. Same week, lock the runner-up into a role they can be proud of. Equity, scope, public respect.
Final 12 to 24 months · relay Overlap. Successor runs more and more of the real work. Outgoing president becomes guide, not hero. Clients are walked across. The sitting president's next chapter is already live so they can actually let go.

What I would not do

  • Drop in a finished outsider as president because the calendar got short.
  • Name one heir five years out and hope the others stay inspired.
  • Run a public horse race.
  • Confuse loyalty with readiness.
  • Let the sitting president pick their clone unchallenged.
  • Treat succession as an HR document. It is a culture document.

Why this belongs in this house

The CCO's job is not to manage six companies. It is to build six leaders who build companies that last. Succession is that sentence made practical. Ecosystems over egosystems. Presidents with real authority and real equity. Relational equity instead of a blunt org chart. Character first.
